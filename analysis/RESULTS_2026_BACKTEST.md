# 2026 Bracket Backtest — Does the Model Have Real Edge?

Generated by cross-referencing the saved model predictions against the actual
2026 NCAA tournament results. Scripts: `analysis/full_backtest.py`,
`analysis/build_full_kenpom_predictions.py`, `analysis/build_full_nonkenpom_predictions.py`,
`analysis/ground_truth_2026.py`. Raw output: `reports/2026_backtest/*.csv`.

**Update:** the non-KenPom pipeline *was* rebuilt against the real 2026
bracket after all (see Section 5) — `team_season_full.parquet` turned out to
already have correct Season=2026 data on disk, so no pipeline rebuild was
actually needed; the fix was just writing the full pairwise prediction table
that `predict_target_season.py` computes internally but never saves to
disk. All numbers below now include a real, non-stale non-KenPom-vs-2026
comparison.

## TL;DR

- On the **large historical held-out test** (401 games, 6 seasons), both
  pipelines are close to a **seed-only logistic regression**. The non-KenPom
  GBT edges it out narrowly (logloss 0.575 vs 0.578). The KenPom `kp_only`
  GBT ties it almost exactly (0.599 vs 0.599). `kp_seed` is slightly worse
  (0.607).
- On the **actual 2026 bracket** (67 games, one season, small sample), results
  diverge by pipeline. The seed-only baseline **beat both KenPom models**
  (logloss 0.513 vs 0.663 for `kp_seed`). But the **non-KenPom raw GBT beat
  every baseline**, including seed-only (logloss 0.505 vs 0.513) — the one
  clean case in this whole backtest of a trained model outright winning on
  the real, current-year bracket.
- The Monte Carlo percentile check (see below) puts the 2026 bracket's
  deterministic score at the **41st percentile** (KenPom `kp_seed`) / **45th
  percentile** (non-KenPom) of each model's own implied probability
  distribution — i.e., roughly average-to-below-average luck by the model's
  own reckoning, not a standout year, even for the pipeline that won on
  logloss. Across the 6 truly held-out backtest seasons the mean percentile
  is **69.6** (KenPom) / **64.5** (non-KenPom), meaning the "pick the
  favorite" strategy usually does land above the middle of its own
  random-draw distribution — that's the real, modest edge. But 2026 itself
  was not one of the strong years by this measure for either pipeline.
- **Conclusion: the edge is real but thin, and it lives almost entirely in
  one strong feature (efficiency margin or seed).** A Kaggle leaderboard
  percentile in the 90s is consistent with either "the model is meaningfully
  better than the field" or "an average-to-below-average year for a
  modestly-better-than-chalk model plus favorable bracket variance" — the
  backtest can't fully separate those, but 2026's own within-model
  percentile (41st–45th, below the historical 65th-70th average) argues for
  the latter more than the former for *this specific year*, even in the one
  pipeline (non-KenPom) that shows a genuine logloss win.

## 1. Log loss vs. baselines

### Large locked-test set (no 2026 dependency — this is the statistically
meaningful comparison)

**KenPom pipeline**, seasons `[2018, 2019, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024]`, n=401:

| Model | Logloss | Brier | Accuracy |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Baseline: seed-only logistic | 0.5988 | 0.2066 | 0.6708 |
| Baseline: Elo-only logistic | 0.6475 | 0.2295 | 0.5810 |
| Baseline: 50/50 coin flip | 0.6931 | 0.2500 | 0.5162 |
| **KenPom `kp_only` GBT (raw)** | **0.5987** | **0.2035** | **0.6808** |
| **KenPom `kp_seed` GBT (raw)** | **0.6071** | **0.2058** | **0.6733** |

**Non-KenPom pipeline**, seasons `[2019, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025]`, n=401:

| Model | Logloss | Brier | Accuracy |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Baseline: seed-only logistic | 0.5784 | 0.1981 | 0.6958 |
| Baseline: Elo-only logistic | 0.6323 | 0.2233 | 0.5810 |
| Baseline: 50/50 coin flip | 0.6931 | 0.2500 | 0.5362 |
| **Non-KenPom GBT raw** | **0.5754** | **0.1975** | **0.6958** |
| Non-KenPom GBT + sigmoid calibration | 0.5829 | 0.2003 | 0.6658 |

**Reading this honestly:** seed alone gets you almost all the way there.
`kp_only` ties the seed baseline on logloss (0.5987 vs 0.5988); the
non-KenPom raw GBT beats it by 0.003 logloss. That is a real but very small
edge — not the kind of gap that would separate a top-6% leaderboard finish
from the pack on skill alone. Interestingly, adding seed as an explicit
*feature* to the KenPom GBT (`kp_seed`) makes logloss slightly worse than
leaving it out (`kp_only`) — the GBT already recovers most of seed's signal
from the efficiency-margin features, and the redundant seed feature adds
noise. This matches `docs/RESULTS_SUMMARY.md`'s existing ablation finding
that seed features "do not consistently improve final-test logloss."

### Actual 2026 bracket (small sample, n=67 — directional only)

The originally-saved `reports/target_predictions.csv` / `game_by_game_results.csv`
turned out to be stale for the *full pairwise* comparison — `predict_target_season.py`
computes the full Season/TeamA/TeamB/Pred table internally but never actually
writes it to disk (`OUT_PATH_DEFAULT` / `args.out` are dead code — only the
bracket-path subset gets saved). `analysis/build_full_nonkenpom_predictions.py`
reruns the same feature-building + calibration logic and confirms
`team_season_full.parquet` already has correct, current Season=2026 data
(seed_num=1 for Duke/Michigan/Florida/Arizona, matching the real bracket) —
so no pipeline rebuild was needed, just generating the missing output file.
All 67 games have full coverage for all five rows below.

| Model | Logloss | Brier | Accuracy |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **Non-KenPom GBT raw** | **0.5046** | **0.1725** | **0.6866** |
| Baseline: seed-only logistic | 0.5128 | 0.1708 | 0.7015 |
| Non-KenPom GBT + sigmoid calibration | 0.5340 | 0.1790 | 0.7313 |
| Baseline: Elo-only logistic | 0.6654 | 0.2313 | 0.6567 |
| KenPom `kp_seed` GBT (raw) | 0.6628 | 0.2249 | 0.6716 |
| Baseline: 50/50 coin flip | 0.6931 | 0.2500 | 0.5522 |
| KenPom `kp_only` GBT (raw) | 0.7258 | 0.2359 | 0.6567 |

This is the most interesting single result in the whole backtest: on the
real 2026 bracket, the **non-KenPom raw GBT beats seed-only on logloss**
(0.5046 vs 0.5128) — modest, but real, and it's the *only* trained model in
this entire analysis (historical or 2026) that clears the seed baseline on a
genuine out-of-sample bracket rather than tying or losing to it. The
sigmoid-calibrated version trades some logloss for the best accuracy of any
model tested (73.1%) — calibration smooths out a few overconfident calls at
the cost of sharper predictions. Meanwhile both KenPom models lost to
seed-only this year. 2026 was chalkier than the historical average overall
(seed-only accuracy 70.1% is strong), which is exactly the kind of year
where a model has to bring more than seed-correlated signal to win — and the
non-KenPom model did, barely; the KenPom models didn't.

## 2. Brier score + calibration curve

Brier scores are in the tables above (lower is better; 0.25 is the coin-flip
floor). Calibration curves use the same locked-test sets, binned by the
predicted probability assigned to the *favorite* in each game (so "70%" means
"the team the model favored at 70% confidence," win or lose):

**KenPom `kp_seed`** (n=401):

| Predicted range | n games | Avg. predicted | Actual win rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 50–60% | 88 | 55.0% | 43.2% |
| 60–70% | 73 | 64.8% | 69.9% |
| 70–80% | 72 | 75.6% | 62.5% |
| 80–90% | 88 | 85.4% | 73.9% |
| 90–100% | 80 | 94.8% | 88.8% |

**KenPom `kp_only`** (n=401):

| Predicted range | n games | Avg. predicted | Actual win rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 50–60% | 99 | 55.1% | 50.5% |
| 60–70% | 60 | 65.5% | 58.3% |
| 70–80% | 85 | 75.6% | 68.2% |
| 80–90% | 74 | 85.3% | 77.0% |
| 90–100% | 83 | 94.5% | 87.9% |

**Non-KenPom** (n=401):

| Predicted range | n games | Avg. predicted | Actual win rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 50–60% | 177 | 54.6% | 49.7% |
| 60–70% | 118 | 64.3% | 72.0% |
| 70–80% | 61 | 74.6% | 83.6% |
| 80–90% | 32 | 84.6% | 93.8% |
| 90–100% | 13 | 91.7% | 100.0% |

**When you said 70%, did it hit 70%?** Roughly, with real bin-to-bin noise
that's expected at n≈13–177 per bin. The 50–60% bin is close to
well-calibrated everywhere (43–55% predicted vs. 43–55% actual — this is the
coin-flip region, as expected). The `kp_seed` model is *slightly
overconfident* in the 70–80% bin (75.6% predicted vs. 62.5% actual — a real
gap, though on only 72 games) and *underconfident* at the top end for the
non-KenPom model (91.7% predicted vs. 100% actual, but n=13 is too small to
read much into). None of these are the several-standard-deviation misses
that would indicate broken calibration; they look like sampling noise around
a genuinely OK-calibrated model, with the caveat that 400 games split into 5
bins gives each bin real width in a binomial sense.

## 3. Percentile distribution across held-out seasons (skill vs. luck)

Methodology: for every actual tournament game in a season, take the model's
predicted win probability for the team that actually played (not the model's
own hypothetical bracket path — this credits the model on every real game
it would have seen, weighted by round value 1-2-4-8-16-32 for R64 through
the championship). The **deterministic score** is what "always pick the
favorite" actually earned that year. The **Monte Carlo distribution** (20,000
draws) simulates what a coin-weighted bracket-filler using the *exact same
probabilities* would have scored by chance alone. The percentile tells you:
given how confident the model actually was, was this year's result better or
worse than what its own uncertainty would typically produce?

| Pipeline | Season | Score | Max | Percentile |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| KenPom `kp_seed` | 2018 | 139 | 192 | 83.4 |
| KenPom `kp_seed` | 2019 | 140 | 192 | 73.7 |
| KenPom `kp_seed` | 2021 | 117 | 194 | 47.4 |
| KenPom `kp_seed` | 2022 | 121 | 192 | 49.9 |
| KenPom `kp_seed` | 2023 | 143 | 192 | 91.4 |
| KenPom `kp_seed` | 2024 | 135 | 192 | 71.9 |
| **KenPom `kp_seed`** | **2026** | **100** | **192** | **41.0** |
| Non-KenPom | 2019 | 132 | 192 | 79.7 |
| Non-KenPom | 2021 | 108 | 194 | 54.7 |
| Non-KenPom | 2022 | 138 | 192 | 94.3 |
| Non-KenPom | 2023 | 93 | 192 | 40.6 |
| Non-KenPom | 2024 | 105 | 192 | 52.3 |
| Non-KenPom | 2025 | 118 | 192 | 65.6 |
| **Non-KenPom** | **2026** | **123** | **192** | **44.8** |

**Mean percentile across the 6 held-out backtest seasons: 69.6 (KenPom),
64.5 (non-KenPom).** That consistent lean above the 50th percentile across
independent seasons is the real, reproducible signature of edge — if the
model had zero skill beyond its own stated uncertainty, the average
percentile across many seasons would center on 50, not 65–70.

**2026 landed at the 41st percentile (KenPom) and 44.8th percentile
(non-KenPom)** — both below their historical averages, and below the
50th-percentile "typical year" mark. This holds even for the non-KenPom
pipeline, which (per Section 1) had the best 2026 logloss of anything tested,
including seed-only. Those two facts aren't contradictory: logloss measures
whether the *stated probabilities* were accurate; this percentile measures
whether *this specific draw of 63 games* landed better or worse than what
those probabilities would typically produce. A well-calibrated model can
still have a below-average-percentile year if slight favorites lost more
often than their probabilities implied — which is what happened here. In
other words: judged by the model's own probabilities, 2026 was an
unremarkable-to-mildly-unlucky year for this specific bracket on both
pipelines, not a standout one. If the real-world result (e.g., a leaderboard
placement in the 90s of percentile) was reported elsewhere as a highlight
outcome, this backtest suggests that placement reflects either (a) the field
of competing brackets that year being weaker/chalkier than the model, rather
than the model itself doing something unusual, or (b) scoring/format
differences between the Kaggle competition's log-loss-based leaderboard and
this points-based bracket scoring. Both are plausible and worth being
upfront about rather than assuming the percentile is a direct skill signal.

*Caveat on methodology:* this scores every actual game independently rather
than simulating a strict single bracket path (where one early miss zeroes
out all downstream credit). It's the standard way to isolate "was the
model's assessed probability, in aggregate, better than its own admitted
uncertainty" from "did this one single-elimination bracket path happen to
survive," and it's more statistically stable with only ~63-67 games per
season. It will read slightly more forgiving than strict single-bracket
ESPN-pool scoring, which is worth keeping in mind if this number is compared
directly to a pool-style leaderboard percentile.

## 4. Feature importance

From `reports/permutation_importance_kenpom.csv` (mean logloss degradation
when a feature is shuffled — bigger means more important):

| Rank | Feature | Mean Δ logloss |
|---:|---|---:|
| 1 | `kp_adj_em_diff` (adjusted efficiency margin) | 0.0752 |
| 2 | `kp_adj_oe_diff` (adjusted offensive efficiency) | 0.0236 |
| 3 | `kp_adj_de_diff` (adjusted defensive efficiency) | 0.0150 |
| 4 | `abs_kp_adj_em_diff` | 0.0125 |
| 5 | `kp_to_pct_diff` (turnover rate) | 0.0108 |

From `reports/permutation_importance.csv` (non-KenPom pipeline):

| Rank | Feature | Mean Δ logloss |
|---:|---|---:|
| 1 | `seed_num_diff` | 0.0622 |
| 2 | `avg_margin_diff` | 0.0123 |
| 3 | `opp_margin_avg_diff` (strength-adjusted margin) | 0.0100 |
| 4 | `opp_win_pct_avg_diff` | 0.0057 |
| 5 | `ppg_against_diff` | 0.0019 |

**Why it works:** in the KenPom pipeline, overall adjusted efficiency margin
(offense minus defense, adjusted for opponent strength) dominates — this is
the single number KenPom is built around, and it alone accounts for roughly
3x the next-biggest feature's importance. In the non-KenPom pipeline, the
biggest driver is literally the seed number difference — consistent with
the baseline comparison above showing seed-only regression nearly matching
the full model. Both pipelines agree on the underlying story: **team-quality
margin (however it's measured) is almost the whole signal; style stats
(turnovers, rebounding, free-throw rate) contribute only at the margin.**

## 5. Data integrity finding (worth knowing, not a modeling flaw)

While rebuilding predictions for this backtest, `csv_folder/MNCAATourneySeeds.csv`
turned out to have two different season-numbering conventions layered on
top of each other:

- `kenpom_team_season.parquet` follows **KenPom's own convention** (a season
  is labeled by the year it *starts* — the 2025-26 season's team quality is
  "Season 2025").
- `team_season_full.parquet` (non-KenPom) follows **straight Kaggle
  convention** (a season is labeled by the year of its *tournament*).
- `MNCAATourneySeeds.csv`'s "Season 2025" row **used to** hold the real 2026
  bracket as a placeholder (matching KenPom's convention, since that's what
  the original prediction run needed), but has since been overwritten with
  the true historical 2024-25 bracket. A fresh "Season 2026" row was added
  separately holding the real 2026 bracket (Kaggle convention).

Net effect: `reports/game_by_game_results_kenpom.csv` is reproducible today
(this backtest does so, splitting "Season 2025 for team quality" from
"Season 2026 for the bracket field" — see `analysis/build_full_kenpom_predictions.py`).

**Update — this turned out to be fixable without a pipeline rebuild.**
`reports/target_predictions.csv` on disk is still a stale artifact (it
references a bracket field that only shares 36 of 68 teams with the real
2026 field, and appears to predate even the placeholder-2025 stage of the
KenPom side's history — likely a leftover from an earlier, unrelated draft
run). But `team_season_full.parquet` itself was **not** stale: checking it
directly showed Season=2026 rows already exist with the correct real-2026
seeds (seed_num=1 for Duke/Michigan/Florida/Arizona) and current-season
Elo/win-pct/margin features — 365 teams, 68 of them seeded, matching the
real bracket exactly. The actual bug was narrower than first diagnosed: `predict_target_season.py`
computes the full pairwise Season/TeamA/TeamB/Pred table in memory (variable
`out`) but **never writes it to disk** — `OUT_PATH_DEFAULT` and `args.out`
are dead code, the function only calls `results.to_csv(...)` for the
bracket-path subset. `analysis/build_full_nonkenpom_predictions.py` reruns
the same feature-building and sigmoid-calibration logic already in
`predict_target_season.py` and writes the missing full table
(`reports/full_pairwise_predictions_nonkenpom.csv`, 2278 rows, full 68-team
coverage), which sanity-checks bit-for-bit against the probabilities already
in `game_by_game_results.csv` for every matchup that overlaps (e.g. Duke vs.
Siena: 0.8561093617840477 in both). This is what made the real Section 1
non-KenPom-vs-2026 comparison possible. This is purely a data-hygiene /
missing-output-file issue in the pipeline, not a modeling problem.

## Bottom line for the resume claim

The honest version: **there is a small, real, historically-reproducible
edge over seed-only and Elo-only baselines** (roughly 65th-70th percentile
average outcome across held-out seasons, and a genuine if modest logloss
edge over baselines on 400+ historical games in both pipelines). That edge
is driven almost entirely by one strong feature (adjusted efficiency margin,
or seed as its proxy) rather than the model's added complexity.
**2026 specifically was a mixed, below-average year by the model's own
probabilities on both pipelines** — KenPom lost to seed-only outright
(logloss 0.663 vs 0.513) and landed at the 41st percentile of its own
implied distribution; non-KenPom won on logloss (0.505 vs 0.513) but still
landed at only the 44.8th percentile, below its own 64.5-average across
held-out seasons. So even the one pipeline that "won" this year did so by a
thin logloss margin while still having a below-typical bracket-scoring
outcome. If a headline percentile (like a 94th-percentile leaderboard
finish) came from this season, the backtest here says to treat that number
with real skepticism about how much of it is model skill vs. bracket
variance — which is exactly the kind of self-aware distinction that's worth
saying out loud.
